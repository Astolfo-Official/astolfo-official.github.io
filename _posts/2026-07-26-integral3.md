## Where do the Fresnel integrals come from?

A monochromatic scalar wave of wavelength $\lambda$ has wavenumber $k=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda},$ and propagation over a distance $r$ contributes the phase $e^{ikr}.$ Let $\xi$ be a coordinate in an aperture plane and $x$ a coordinate in an
observation plane a distance $z$ away. Their separation is

$$
r=\sqrt{z^2+(x-\xi)^2}.
$$

When $|x-\xi|\ll z$, the binomial expansion gives the **Fresnel
approximation**

$$
r
=z\sqrt{1+\frac{(x-\xi)^2}{z^2}}
\simeq z+\frac{(x-\xi)^2}{2z}.
$$

Therefore,

$$
e^{ikr}
\simeq e^{ikz}
\exp\left[\frac{ik}{2z}(x-\xi)^2\right].
$$

Apart from an overall prefactor, the Huygens--Fresnel diffraction formula is
then

$$
U(x,z)\propto
\int_{\text{aperture}}
U_0(\xi)
\exp\left[\frac{ik}{2z}(x-\xi)^2\right]\,\mathrm{d}\xi.
$$

For uniform illumination, observation at $x=0$, and an aperture extending from
$\xi=0$ to $\xi=L$,

$$
U(0,z)\propto
\int_0^L
\exp\left(\frac{i\pi\xi^2}{\lambda z}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\xi.
$$

Introduce the dimensionless variables

$$
t=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\lambda z}}\,\xi,
\qquad
u=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\lambda z}}\,L.
$$

The diffraction field becomes

$$
U(0,z)\propto
\sqrt{\frac{\lambda z}{2}}
\int_0^u e^{i\pi t^2/2}\,\mathrm{d}t.
$$

Using Euler's formula,

$$
e^{i\pi t^2/2}
=\cos\left(\frac{\pi t^2}{2}\right)
+i\sin\left(\frac{\pi t^2}{2}\right),
$$

we obtain

$$
U(0,z)\propto
\sqrt{\frac{\lambda z}{2}}
\left[\mathcal{C}(u)+i\mathcal{S}(u)\right], \qquad
\mathcal{C}(u)
=\int_0^u\cos\left(\frac{\pi t^2}{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}t,
\qquad
\mathcal{S}(u)
=\int_0^u\sin\left(\frac{\pi t^2}{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}t.
$$

These are the **Fresnel integrals**. Their parametric plot

$$
u\longmapsto\bigl(\mathcal{C}(u),\mathcal{S}(u)\bigr)
$$

is called the **Cornu spiral**.

After rescaling once more, it is enough to evaluate

$$
F=\int_0^\infty e^{ix^2}\,\mathrm{d}x
$$

and hence its real and imaginary parts,

$$
C=\int_0^\infty\cos(x^2)\,\mathrm{d}x,
\qquad
S=\int_0^\infty\sin(x^2)\,\mathrm{d}x.
$$

These integrals are not absolutely convergent, because
$|e^{ix^2}|=1$. They nevertheless converge as improper oscillatory
integrals. Indeed, integration by parts gives, for $B>A>0$,

$$
\int_A^B e^{ix^2}\,\mathrm{d}x
=\int_A^B\frac{1}{2ix}\,\mathrm{d}\left(e^{ix^2}\right)
=\left[\frac{e^{ix^2}}{2ix}\right]_A^B
+\frac{1}{2i}\int_A^B\frac{e^{ix^2}}{x^2}\,\mathrm{d}x.
$$

The right-hand side tends uniformly to zero as $A\to\infty$, so the Cauchy
criterion proves convergence.

## 1. Schwinger parameterization

Start with the definition of the Gamma function:

$$
\Gamma(\nu)
=\int_0^\infty t^{\nu-1}e^{-t}\,\mathrm{d}t,
\qquad \operatorname{Re}\nu>0.
$$

For the moment, let $A>0$ and make the substitution $t=As$ and $\mathrm{d}t=A\,\mathrm{d}s$. Then

$$
\Gamma(\nu)
=\int_0^\infty
(As)^{\nu-1}e^{-As}A\,\mathrm{d}s
=A^\nu\int_0^\infty
s^{\nu-1}e^{-As}\,\mathrm{d}s.
$$

Dividing by $A^\nu\Gamma(\nu)$ gives the **Schwinger
parameterization**

$$
\frac{1}{A^\nu}
=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\nu)}
\int_0^\infty s^{\nu-1}e^{-As}\,\mathrm{d}s,
\qquad
\operatorname{Re}A>0,\quad \operatorname{Re}\nu>0.
$$

The derivation above used real $A>0$. With the principal branch of $A^\nu$,
both sides are analytic in the right half-plane, so the identity extends to
every complex $A$ with $\operatorname{Re}A>0$.

For the Fresnel integral, a small positive regulator is essential. Define

$$
F_\varepsilon
=\int_0^\infty e^{-(\varepsilon-i)x^2}\,\mathrm{d}x,
\qquad \varepsilon>0.
$$

Unlike the original integral, $F_\varepsilon$ is absolutely convergent. Set
$s=x^2$, so that $x=\sqrt{s}$ and
$\mathrm{d}x=\frac12s^{-1/2}\,\mathrm{d}s$. Then

$$
F_\varepsilon
=\frac12\int_0^\infty
s^{-1/2}e^{-(\varepsilon-i)s}\,\mathrm{d}s.
$$

Apply the Schwinger identity with

$$
\nu=\frac12,
\qquad
A=\varepsilon-i.
$$

Since $\operatorname{Re}(\varepsilon-i)=\varepsilon>0$ and
$\Gamma(\frac12)=\sqrt{\pi}$, we obtain

$$
F_\varepsilon=\frac12\Gamma\left(\frac12\right)(\varepsilon-i)^{-1/2}=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}(\varepsilon-i)^{-1/2}.
$$

We use the principal square root, with argument in $(-\pi,\pi)$. Writing

$$
\varepsilon-i
=\sqrt{1+\varepsilon^2}\,
e^{-i\arctan(1/\varepsilon)}
\quad \Rightarrow \quad
(\varepsilon-i)^{-1/2}
=(1+\varepsilon^2)^{-1/4}
e^{\frac{i}{2}\arctan(1/\varepsilon)}.
$$

As $\varepsilon\to0^+$,

$$
\lim_{\varepsilon\to0^+}F_\varepsilon
=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}e^{i\pi/4}
=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2\sqrt{2}}(1+i).
$$

It remains to connect this Abel-regularized limit to the original improper
integral. For a fixed $R$,

$$
\lim_{\varepsilon\to0^+}
\int_0^R e^{-(\varepsilon-i)x^2}\,\mathrm{d}x
=\int_0^R e^{ix^2}\,\mathrm{d}x
$$

by dominated convergence. The tails can be controlled uniformly for small
$\varepsilon$ by integration by parts:

$$
\int_R^\infty e^{-(\varepsilon-i)x^2}\,\mathrm{d}x
=\int_R^\infty
\frac{-1}{2(\varepsilon-i)x}\,
\mathrm{d}\left(e^{-(\varepsilon-i)x^2}\right)
=\frac{e^{-(\varepsilon-i)R^2}}{2(\varepsilon-i)R}
-\frac{1}{2(\varepsilon-i)}
\int_R^\infty\frac{e^{-(\varepsilon-i)x^2}}{x^2}\,\mathrm{d}x.
$$

For $0<\varepsilon\leq1$, its absolute value is at most $1/R$. Thus we may
first choose $R$ large and then let $\varepsilon\to0^+$. This proves that the
regulated limit equals the ordinary Fresnel integral:

$$
F=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}e^{i\pi/4}.
$$

Taking real and imaginary parts yields

$$
\boxed{
\int_0^\infty\cos(x^2)\,\mathrm{d}x
=\int_0^\infty\sin(x^2)\,\mathrm{d}x
=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{8}}
}
$$

## 2. Complex-analysis method

The same result follows geometrically by rotating the contour through an angle
of $\pi/4$. Consider the entire function

$$
f(z)=e^{iz^2}
$$

on the positively oriented boundary of the sector with vertices
$0$, $R$, and $Re^{i\pi/4}$. Denote the circular arc by

$$
\gamma_R(\theta)=Re^{i\theta},
\qquad 0\leq\theta\leq\frac{\pi}{4}.
$$

<img src="/assets/img/blog_figures/20260726_fresnel_contour_rotation.png" alt="Positively oriented sector contour rotating the Fresnel integral from the real axis to the ray at angle pi over four" style="width: 68%; height: auto; display: block; margin: 1.2rem auto;">

Since $f$ is entire, Cauchy's integral theorem gives

$$
\int_0^R e^{ix^2}\,\mathrm{d}x
+\int_{\gamma_R}e^{iz^2}\,\mathrm{d}z
-\int_0^R e^{i(re^{i\pi/4})^2}e^{i\pi/4}\,\mathrm{d}r
=0.
$$

On the diagonal ray, $(re^{i\pi/4})^2=ir^2$, so

$$
e^{i(re^{i\pi/4})^2}=e^{-r^2}.
$$

Consequently,

$$
\int_0^R e^{ix^2}\,\mathrm{d}x
=e^{i\pi/4}\int_0^R e^{-r^2}\,\mathrm{d}r
-\int_{\gamma_R}e^{iz^2}\,\mathrm{d}z.
$$

We now show that the arc contribution vanishes. On $z=Re^{i\theta}$,

$$
\left|e^{iz^2}\right|
=e^{-R^2\sin(2\theta)}.
$$

Because $\sin(2\theta)\geq4\theta/\pi$ for
$0\leq\theta\leq\pi/4$,

$$
\left|\int_{\gamma_R}e^{iz^2}\,\mathrm{d}z\right|
\leq R\int_0^{\pi/4}e^{-R^2\sin(2\theta)}\,\mathrm{d}\theta
\leq R\int_0^{\pi/4}e^{-4R^2\theta/\pi}\,\mathrm{d}\theta
=\frac{\pi}{4R}\left(1-e^{-R^2}\right)
\longrightarrow0.
$$

Letting $R\to\infty$ and using the Gaussian integral

$$
\int_0^\infty e^{-r^2}\,\mathrm{d}r=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}
$$

therefore gives

$$
F=e^{i\pi/4}\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2},
$$

in agreement with the Schwinger-parameter calculation.

The contour rotation also explains the phase $e^{i\pi/4}$. Along the real
axis, $e^{iz^2}$ oscillates without decaying. Rotating
$z=re^{i\theta}$ changes its magnitude to
$e^{-r^2\sin(2\theta)}$; at $\theta=\pi/4$, the oscillatory exponential becomes
the decaying Gaussian $e^{-r^2}$, while the line element contributes
$\mathrm{d}z=e^{i\pi/4}\,\mathrm{d}r$.

## 3. Common normalization and a scaled formula

Many texts define the Fresnel functions by

$$
\mathcal{C}(u)=\int_0^u\cos\left(\frac{\pi t^2}{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}t,
\qquad
\mathcal{S}(u)=\int_0^u\sin\left(\frac{\pi t^2}{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}t.
$$

Using $x=\sqrt{\pi/2}\,t$ in the result above gives

$$
\mathcal{C}(\infty)=\mathcal{S}(\infty)=\frac12.
$$

More generally, for $a>0$, the substitution $u=\sqrt{a}\,x$ gives

$$
\int_0^\infty e^{iax^2}\,\mathrm{d}x
=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2\sqrt{a}}e^{i\pi/4},
$$

and hence

$$
\boxed{
\int_0^\infty\cos(ax^2)\,\mathrm{d}x
=\int_0^\infty\sin(ax^2)\,\mathrm{d}x
=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{8a}}
}
$$
